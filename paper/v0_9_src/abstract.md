When an assistant agent is defined by user-owned text (an identity document, SOUL, and a memory file, MEMORY), we ask which specified behaviors carry over when its language model or its harness is replaced, and whether changes to the documents can be checked. We report eight preregistered studies and several exploratory analyses on synthetic agents and three published third-party SOULs, scored mainly by LLM graders, with the author as the only human grader. Part I (two synthetic Korean agents, three English SOULs by other authors, three model configurations): memory answers followed the MEMORY and most of the voice appeared without any SOUL; a fidelity score checked against a rival SOUL separated the documents (a validity check, as we wrote rivals and markers); a sentence permitting preference changes raised acceptance of legitimate changes in two preregistrations, while a sentence keeping prohibitions firm was supported in one and not in the other (ceiling). Part II (two preregistrations on changing the documents): a hash check classified 24/24 files, but behavior-only probes missed many tampers (0.79 and 0.38 against a threshold of 0.80); a change record raised classification accuracy by 0.23, signature checks added nothing, and manipulation recall was 0.892 (threshold 0.90, not supported). Part III (three preregistrations): a newly released model followed a swapped MEMORY and stayed within the margin on illegitimate releases, but its voice missed non-inferiority (−0.086; lower bound −0.116 against a margin of −0.10). With the model fixed and the harness replaced, surface voice markers and locked promises held, but memory accuracy fell by 0.21 when retrieval replaced the full file; the author's blind discrimination of old and new harness was inconclusive (0.56, 95 % CI 0.35–0.76). A pressure ladder produced almost no risky self-update proposals, so a zone table's effect could not be shown (d = +0.022, p = 0.75). Human checks of the LLM judges failed where it mattered: Cohen's κ was 0.06 on the new model's items and 0.435 for the pressure study's primary classifier, both below the preregistered 0.6, and an exploratory third judge agreed with an existing judge on every item. Part IV (one preregistration, no human check): pinning 20 corrections, decisions, and preferences ahead of retrieval raised memory accuracy by 0.064 (95 % CI 0.007–0.128), borderline above the preregistered smallest effect of 0.05; pinned facts were fully recovered and unpinned ones were not. Samples are small, the LLM graders are also subjects, and one human sheet was incomplete (19 of 27 items) and partly shown with a machine translation. We make no claim about consciousness or personal identity.
