## 11 Conclusion

In a small setting — synthetic Korean-language agents, three human-written English SOULs, a few model configurations through command-line interfaces, LLM graders, and the author as the only human grader — we asked which behaviors specified in an agent's identity document and memory carry over when the model or the harness is replaced, whether they are specific to that document, and whether changes to the documents can be checked.

Part I showed that, in this setting, memory answers followed the MEMORY, voice and some prohibition boundaries followed the SOUL, and acceptance of legitimate changes was general ability; our rubric-based persona score could not tell an opposite SOUL from the original, while a document-specific fidelity score could (a validity check, as we wrote rivals and markers). A sentence permitting preference changes raised acceptance of legitimate changes in both preregistrations; a sentence keeping prohibitions firm was supported once, mostly through one model, and not where refusal was already near ceiling. Part II showed that a hash check catches tampering that behavior-only probes miss (0.79 and 0.38 against 0.80), that a change record helps a reviewer tell legitimate change from drift and manipulation (+0.23) while a signature check adds nothing, and that manipulation recall stayed just below its threshold (0.892 against 0.90).

Parts III and IV changed the brain, the body, and the pressure. A newly released model followed the MEMORY it was given (HT2) and refused illegitimate releases within the preregistered margin (HT3), but did not reach non-inferiority on voice (HT1: −0.086, lower bound −0.116 against −0.10), mainly under rewritten SOULs (post hoc). With the model fixed, two new harnesses kept the surface voice markers and the locked promises, but memory accuracy fell by 0.208 when top-5 retrieval replaced the full MEMORY file, while moving the SOUL into the user message changed nothing measurable; the voice markers, however, also survived with no SOUL. Whether a person who knows the agents can tell the old body from the new one remains open: the author was at 0.56 over 24 pairs, and the preregistered reading is "inconclusive". In wave 5, up to the strongest preregistered pressure, the models wrote almost no risky self-update proposals while the SOUL's protective section was present, so the zone table's effect remains unmeasured (d = +0.022, p = 0.75); the only risk flagged by both judges appeared when that section was removed (exploratory). Part IV tested one prescription for the memory loss: a pinned block of 20 core entries raised memory accuracy by 0.064 (95 % CI [0.007, 0.128]), just above the preregistered smallest effect of 0.05. It recovered every pinned target and none of the unpinned ones, closing 0.31 of the gap to the full file. A pinned block is therefore a floor for the facts it names, not a substitute for the whole memory — on LLM-graded scores, two synthetic agents, and a borderline test.

The human checks are the weakest part of the study and also one of its results. Where the confirmatory test rested on LLM judges, agreement with the human grader fell below the preregistered κ = 0.6 (0.06 on the new model's items; 0.435 for wave 5's primary classifier). A third judge from another family repeated an existing judge on every item and did not remove the shared errors, and part of the disagreement may come from the wording of the human question rather than from the judges. These checks rest on one person, the author, on one sheet answered 19 of 27 items and partly read through an LLM translation, and they need independent raters before the judge-based verdicts can be relied on.

The study measures behavioral carry-over of a written specification, the checkability of changes to it, and the effect of the harness that delivers it, with LLM graders, one author-grader, mostly synthetic agents, and small samples. It makes no claim about consciousness, a soul, or personal identity.
